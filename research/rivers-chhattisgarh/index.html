Overview

A river is not a line on a map. It is a conversation.

The Rivers of Chhattisgarh programme is Meer Foundation's longest-running field research line. Since 2019, our team has walked the eight major river systems of the state — Sakri, Mahanadi, Shivnath, Hasdeo, Indravati, Arpa, Jonk, and Pairi — to document what is happening to them, who is being heard, and who is being ignored.

The walking method

Each river is walked end-to-end by a 3–5 person team over 6–14 weeks. The team camps in riparian villages, conducts household surveys, takes water samples at every confluence, and records oral histories with elder fishers and farmers. The walk produces a primary dataset; the report is its interpretation. The walk is the research; the report is its artefact.

Field Observations

What the rivers told us, in their own language

Six headline findings from the eight river walks. Each finding is backed by primary data — water samples, household surveys, and oral histories — published in the relevant river report.

Dissolved oxygen crashes

Shivnath downstream of Bhilai: DO dropped to 1.8 mg/L in May 2024 — below the 5 mg/L threshold for most fish species.

Mahseer run still intact

Pairi river's monsoon mahseer run continues — 14 villages reported active spawning in 2024. The reference is holding.

Riparian forest loss

Sakri lost 11% of riparian canopy in 5 years. Hasdeo lost 23% in the same period. Both are reversible if acted on now.

Floodplain encroachment

Mahanadi floodplain between Raipur and Mahasamund: 38% of the 100-year floodplain is now built-on. Flood risk is structural.

Adivasi voice missing

In Hasdeo, of 12 villages surveyed, 9 reported never having been consulted by the mining company that operates upstream of their water source.

Sand mining pressure

Sakri and Pairi both face mechanised sand mining. Manual sand mining by riparian villages is sustainable; mechanised extraction is not.

“I have fished this river for forty years. The mahseer used to come in Bhado (August–September). Now it comes in Asoj (October) — sometimes not at all. The river is waiting longer for the rain that no longer comes on time.”

— Pairi river elder, recorded during the 2023 walk. Quoted with permission.
